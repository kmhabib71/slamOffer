# CHAPTER 7: V - VALIDATE WITH REAL PEOPLE

_"Validation isn't about proving you're right. It's about discovering what's true. And the truth can only be found in real conversations with real people who have real problems."_

May 6th, 2022. 11:47 AM.

I'm sitting in my car outside a Starbucks, palms sweating, staring at my phone. I have a scheduled call with Marcus, a freelance React developer who responded to my Twitter post about pricing struggles.

This is my first real validation call.

I have no idea what I'm doing. I've watched YouTube videos about "customer interviews" and read blog posts about "validation frameworks," but theory and practice are different animals.

My English isn't perfect. I'm an introvert who gets anxious talking to strangers. And I'm about to ask someone to give me 30 minutes of their time to talk about their problems.

But this is the moment everything changes. This is where ideas meet reality.

**THE VALIDATION PARADOX**

Here's what nobody tells you about validation: it's not about getting people to say "yes, I'd buy this."

It's about getting people to tell you their truth.

And the truth is often messy, complicated, and different from what you expected.

**The Facebook Course Validation Mistake**

When I was building "Build a Social Network in 20 Days from Scratch: PHP+MYSQL, JS," I thought I was doing validation.

**My "validation" process:**

- Posted in Facebook groups: "Would you buy a course on building Facebook?"
- Got 23 "sounds interesting" responses
- Concluded: "The market wants this!"

**What I actually validated:**

- People will be polite when you ask leading questions
- "Sounds interesting" doesn't mean "I'll pay for it"
- Generic enthusiasm isn't specific demand

**What I should have asked:**

- "What's the biggest challenge you're facing learning web development?"
- "What's the most recent course you actually purchased?"
- "What would need to be true for you to spend $200 on a development course?"

**THE VALIDATION MINDSET SHIFT**

Most founders approach validation like a sales call. They're trying to convince people to want their solution.

Real validation is the opposite. You're trying to understand if people actually have the problem you think they have.

**Sales mindset:** "Here's my solution, do you want it?"
**Validation mindset:** "Here's a problem I think exists, do you actually have it?"

**Sales mindset:** "My tool will save you time writing proposals"
**Validation mindset:** "Tell me about the last time you had to write a proposal"

**THE VALIDATION CONVERSATION FRAMEWORK**

After 47 validation calls, I developed a framework that works even for introverts who hate talking to strangers:

**Part 1: The Problem Discovery (15 minutes)**

- Tell me about the last time you had to [do the thing related to your solution]
- What was frustrating about that experience?
- How did you solve it?
- What would have made it easier?

**Part 2: The Current Solution Analysis (10 minutes)**

- What do you currently use to solve this problem?
- What do you like about that solution?
- What do you wish was different?
- How much time/money does this problem cost you?

**Part 3: The Future State Exploration (5 minutes)**

- If you could wave a magic wand, what would the ideal solution look like?
- What would need to be true for you to switch from your current solution?
- What's the biggest barrier to solving this problem?

**MY FIRST VALIDATION CALL**

11:47 AM. I dial Marcus's number.

"Hey Marcus, thanks for agreeing to chat. I'm not selling anything, I'm just trying to understand how freelance developers handle pricing and proposals."

**Me:** "Tell me about the last time you had to write a proposal for a client."

**Marcus:** "Oh man, just last week. I spent like 3 hours on this proposal for a React dashboard. The client wanted a 'simple' analytics tool, but I knew it would be complex. I had no idea how to price it."

**Me:** "What was frustrating about that experience?"

**Marcus:** "I kept second-guessing myself. Is $3,000 too much? Too little? I ended up at $2,500 and the client said yes immediately. Which probably means I undercharged."

**Me:** "How do you usually figure out pricing?"

**Marcus:** "I basically guess. I look at similar projects I've done, add some buffer, and hope for the best. It's terrifying."

**Me:** "What would have made it easier?"

**Marcus:** "I wish I had a way to know if my pricing was fair. Like, some framework or calculator or something. I spend more time stressing about the proposal than actually building the thing."

**VALIDATION GOLD.**

This wasn't just confirmation that the problem existed. This was insight into the emotional reality of the problem.

Marcus didn't just need pricing help. He needed confidence. He needed to stop second-guessing himself. He needed to feel like his prices were justified.

**THE VALIDATION QUESTION TYPES**

Through dozens of calls, I learned that different question types reveal different truths:

**Story Questions** (reveal behavior):

- "Tell me about the last time you..."
- "Walk me through your process for..."
- "What happened when you..."

**Emotion Questions** (reveal feelings):

- "What was frustrating about that?"
- "How did that make you feel?"
- "What worries you most about..."

**Frequency Questions** (reveal patterns):

- "How often does this happen?"
- "When was the last time..."
- "How many times per month..."

**Outcome Questions** (reveal value):

- "What would happen if you could solve this?"
- "What would that save you?"
- "What would that be worth?"

**THE VALIDATION REVELATIONS**

The calls revealed truths I never expected:

**Truth #1: Speed wasn't the main issue**
I thought people wanted faster proposal creation. They actually wanted confidence in their pricing.

**Truth #2: Templates weren't enough**
I thought better templates would solve the problem. They needed pricing psychology and negotiation guidance.

**Truth #3: The pain was emotional, not just practical**
I thought this was a productivity problem. It was actually a confidence problem.

**Truth #4: The value was in the outcome, not the process**
I thought they'd pay for better proposals. They'd pay for higher-paying clients.

**THE VALIDATION MISTAKES TO AVOID**

**Mistake #1: Leading Questions**
"Would you buy a tool that helps you write better proposals?"
Everyone says yes to leading questions.

**Better:** "Tell me about your current proposal process."

**Mistake #2: Hypothetical Questions**
"If there was a tool that did X, would you use it?"
Hypothetical questions get hypothetical answers.

**Better:** "What did you do the last time you faced this problem?"

**Mistake #3: Feature Validation**
"Do you think a pricing calculator would be useful?"
Features don't matter until you understand the problem.

**Better:** "How do you currently figure out pricing?"

**Mistake #4: Asking for Compliments**
"What do you think of my idea?"
People are polite. They'll say nice things.

**Better:** "What's the biggest challenge you face with [problem]?"

**THE VALIDATION FORMULA**

Every validation call follows this formula:

**Past Behavior + Current Pain + Future Desire = Validation Insight**

**Past Behavior:** What did they actually do last time?
**Current Pain:** What sucks about the current solution?
**Future Desire:** What would they want instead?

**THE VALIDATION METRICS**

Track these metrics across all your calls:

**Problem Confirmation Rate:** % who confirm they have the problem
**Pain Intensity Score:** 1-10 how painful the problem is
**Frequency Score:** How often they encounter the problem
**Current Solution Cost:** Time + money spent on current solutions
**Desired Outcome Value:** What solving it would be worth

**My GSO tool results:**

- Problem Confirmation: 91%
- Average Pain Intensity: 7.3/10
- Average Frequency: 5.2 proposals/month
- Current Solution Cost: 2.5 hours + $0/proposal
- Desired Outcome Value: $500-2000/month in higher prices

**THE VALIDATION EVOLUTION**

Your validation questions will evolve as you learn:

**Week 1:** "Do you write proposals?" (too basic)
**Week 2:** "Tell me about your proposal process" (better)
**Week 3:** "What's the hardest part about pricing proposals?" (more specific)
**Week 4:** "Walk me through the last time you undercharged" (behavioral)

**THE VALIDATION CONFIDENCE BUILDER**

As an introvert, validation calls terrified me. Here's what helped:

**Preparation:** Write out your questions beforehand
**Practice:** Do 3-5 calls with friends first
**Mindset:** You're helping them by listening, not bothering them
**Focus:** Their problems, not your solution
**Follow-up:** Send a thank-you note after each call

**THE VALIDATION RECRUITING STRATEGY**

Finding people to validate with:

**Strategy 1: Signal Response**
People who responded to your signal posts are perfect validation candidates.

**Strategy 2: Network Leverage**
Ask friends, colleagues, and connections for introductions.

**Strategy 3: Cold Outreach**
DM people who post about related problems on social media.

**Strategy 4: Community Engagement**
Be helpful in relevant communities, then ask for feedback.

**THE VALIDATION INCENTIVE STRUCTURE**

People are more likely to validate if you:

**Offer Value:** "I'll share what I learn with you"
**Respect Time:** "Just 15-20 minutes"
**Be Specific:** "I'm not selling anything, just learning"
**Show Gratitude:** "This would be incredibly helpful"

**THE VALIDATION RED FLAGS**

Stop pursuing an idea if:

**< 60% problem confirmation:** Not enough people have the problem
**< 5/10 pain intensity:** The problem isn't painful enough
**< monthly frequency:** The problem doesn't happen often enough
**< $100/month value:** The solution isn't worth paying for

**THE VALIDATION BREAKTHROUGH MOMENT**

Call #23 with Sarah, a React developer in Austin:

**Me:** "Tell me about the last time you felt confident about your pricing."

**Sarah:** "Honestly? Never. I've been freelancing for 2 years and I still have no idea if I'm charging enough. I see other developers posting about $100/hour rates and I'm charging $40. But I don't know how to justify higher prices."

**Me:** "What would need to change for you to feel confident charging $100/hour?"

**Sarah:** "I'd need to know that my proposals were actually good. Like, that they positioned me as an expert, not just another developer. I'd need to understand the psychology of why clients pay premium prices."

**That's when I knew:** This wasn't just about proposal templates. This was about transformation from underconfident freelancer to premium service provider.

**THE VALIDATION TRANSITION**

After 47 calls, I had enough data to move forward. But validation never really ends.

Every customer conversation is validation. Every support ticket is validation. Every cancellation is validation.

But there's a difference between validation and paralysis.

You validate until you have enough confidence to build, not until you have certainty.

**THE VALIDATION COMMITMENT**

Validation isn't about getting permission to build. It's about understanding what to build.

The goal isn't to hear "yes, I'll buy this." The goal is to understand the problem so deeply that your solution becomes obvious.

**THE VALIDATION TRUTH**

Real validation is uncomfortable because it forces you to confront reality.

Sometimes the reality is that your idea is perfect and people desperately need it.

Sometimes the reality is that your idea needs to be completely reimagined.

Sometimes the reality is that your idea should be abandoned.

All of these realities are gifts.

Because building something nobody wants is the most expensive mistake you can make.

**THE VALIDATION PROMISE**

If you do validation right, you'll never again wonder "Will people buy this?"

You'll know. Because you'll have talked to them.

You'll understand their problems better than they do.

You'll know what they want before they ask for it.

You'll build with confidence instead of hope.

But first, you have to do the work.

You have to have the conversations.

You have to ask the questions.

You have to listen to the answers.

Even when they're not what you want to hear.

Especially when they're not what you want to hear.

Because that's when you learn the most.
